# AI Lead

Status: Agreed direction. No runtime behavior has changed. The collection
format, player controls, and evaluation suite below are the contract for the
first implementation work; model choice stays open until a baseline has been
auditioned.

## Intent

nooise should be able to take a lead turn that feels like somebody is playing
with the song. It needs to hear the progression, leave room, begin an idea,
develop it over several bars, and resolve or hand it back. A stream of
in-scale notes is not sufficient.

The first target is a personal soloist trained on Ian's MIDI playing in
nooise. It has a deliberately small musical world:

- one monophonic Lead part;
- nooise's known tempo, transport, progression, current voiced chord, home
  key, and mode;
- a live, low-latency response;
- a player who can take over at any time.

This is not a plan to train a general-purpose music foundation model. A
thousand hours of intentional MIDI performances is a substantial corpus for
this focused job. It is not the right scale for starting a broad, from-scratch
music model that must learn every genre, instrument, and harmonic system.

## What sustained improvisation means

The desired result has memory at the phrase level. It should recognize an
earlier idea and choose to repeat it, move it, simplify it, answer it, leave
space after it, or resolve it when the harmony calls for a landing. It should
not continuously fill available time because it has learned that note density
reduces prediction loss.

That behavior needs an explicit structure in addition to note prediction.
The initial design separates two jobs:

1. A phrase planner looks at the current section, recent phrases, harmonic
   direction, and a short performance brief. It selects a phrase action such
   as rest, introduce, repeat, vary, answer, climb, release, or resolve.
2. A note generator turns that selected action into pitched MIDI notes, note
   durations, velocities, and silences against the current chord and scale.

The planner keeps a compact motif record. A motif is a played phrase plus
metadata that makes it reusable: onset relative to the bar, contour, rhythm,
register, chord-relative pitch classes, and a phrase identifier. The planner
can retrieve a prior motif and ask for an exact return, transposition,
rhythmic reduction, contour variation, or an answering phrase. The note
generator receives that decision as an input. It does not have to infer an
entire song's musical form from a raw event stream alone.

This is the working answer to long-range continuity. A Transformer-style
model can still provide the learned musical vocabulary, but motif memory and
phrase decisions make continuity observable, controllable, and testable.

## Capture the performances, not only MIDI files

Each take starts before playing and ends after the final release. It captures
one uninterrupted performance session, including rests, false starts,
repetitions, and the section before and after a solo. Do not trim it to only
the attractive bars before preserving the raw take. What happened before a
phrase is often why the phrase makes sense.

The recorder writes two artifacts:

- An append-only raw event log. Every entry has a monotonic timestamp, the
  corresponding musical position, source identity, and the nooise state known
  at that moment. This is the durable source record.
- A compiled training take: standard MIDI for listening and interchange, plus
  versioned JSON sidecar data for the complete nooise context. The compiler is
  deterministic so a dataset can always be rebuilt from the raw take.

The raw log must never be written on the audio callback. MIDI and state events
go through a bounded queue to a recorder worker. A full queue records an
explicit dropped-event marker and reports that the take is unusable; it must
not stall audio or silently manufacture a clean-looking take.

Every event should carry this minimum context:

| Field | Why it is recorded |
| --- | --- |
| Take and session identifiers, schema version, source MIDI device, and source class | Keeps raw human playing separate from generated or replayed notes and makes the data traceable. |
| Monotonic time, sample or engine frame, beat, bar, beat-in-bar, and phrase position | Reconstructs timing despite a tempo change or UI-frame delay. |
| MIDI note-on, note-off, pitch, velocity, channel, and input timestamp | Preserves the performance rather than reducing it to a quantized note list. |
| BPM, swing, clock running state, and time-signature assumptions | Lets a model learn feel relative to the active grid. |
| Progression identity or custom chord definition, chord-window position, current chord symbol, chord pitches, bass root, home key, and mode | Gives the model actual harmony instead of asking it to guess it from the accompaniment. |
| Lead follow mode, octave, glide, pattern state, current active Lead notes, and recent human Lead history | Captures the exact instrument reach and what the player has already said. |
| Arrangement energy and other layer levels or mutes | Makes space and intensity part of the condition, rather than a hidden audio-only fact. |
| A structured solo brief and human annotations | Provides controllable intent and later preference data. |

The sidecar stores full chord pitches as well as names. A chord symbol can be
ambiguous; the voiced notes and bass are what the player actually hears.

MIDI input has a source identity from its first recorded event. Human MIDI is
training material. Generated Lead output, captured-lane playback, and external
clock or accompaniment messages are recorded as separate sources and excluded
from the human-performance corpus unless deliberately labeled for another use.
This avoids a feedback loop where the model learns its own mistakes as though
they were the player's choices.

## Start with structured intent, then add language

Natural-language prompts do not appear automatically in a MIDI dataset. If a
take contains no labels, the system cannot learn what “restless but spacious”
was supposed to mean.

The first recorder uses a small structured brief that can be selected before a
take or changed at a bar boundary:

| Dimension | Initial values |
| --- | --- |
| Space | sparse, open, steady, dense |
| Motion | still, drifting, moving, urgent |
| Tension | settled, leaning, tense, release |
| Register | low, middle, high, roaming |
| Motif behavior | new, repeat, vary, answer, resolve |
| Role | intro, support, lead turn, peak, outro |

After a take, the player can star a phrase, reject a phrase, mark a phrase as
the intended answer to an earlier motif, or write a short free-text note. Do
not force annotation during a performance. The useful first labels are the
ones that take seconds after listening back.

Later, a prompt interpreter can map text into this same brief. It can learn
from paired text and selected values without changing the musical model's
core interface. The performance engine continues to receive bounded fields it
can honor while playing.

## Collection plan

Record broadly inside the exact world the model will play in. The corpus needs
more than long free improvisations over one favorite loop.

- Capture full takes, commonly eight to thirty-two bars, and include some
  longer multi-section takes where an idea returns after a break.
- Cover major, minor, Dorian, Phrygian, and Mixolydian progressions; include
  nooise's built-ins, custom progressions, borrowed chords, and different
  chord lengths.
- Cover slow, medium, and fast tempos; straight and swung time; sparse and
  active arrangements; different Lead reaches and registers.
- Deliberately record call-and-response, motif repetition, variation,
  chromatic approach notes where desired, pauses, endings, and solos that
  stop before the harmony does.
- Keep takes that expose a useful mistake or recovery. They teach timing and
  return behavior, provided they are labeled rather than accidentally mixed
  into a set of intended examples.

A thousand hours is a destination, not a prerequisite. Begin training after
the first carefully reviewed 25 to 50 hours, then again around 100 and 250.
Each checkpoint tells us whether more hours of the same material help, or
whether the corpus lacks a musical situation. The thousand-hour target becomes
valuable because it spans many contexts and repeated personal choices, not
because the number itself guarantees a result.

Split data by complete session and song, never by random bars from the same
take. The validation and listening sets must contain progressions and entire
performances the trainer has not seen. Otherwise a model can appear to have
long-term memory merely by recognizing adjacent chunks from the same session.

Keep raw data private by default. Each compiled dataset manifest names its
source takes, schema version, compiler version, inclusion decision, and any
annotation edits. A model checkpoint records the manifest hash. No training
run should be impossible to reproduce because a folder was hand-edited.

## Model path

The work begins by comparing an existing real-time MIDI model with a small
nooise-specific system.

Notochord is the first baseline to audition. It is a real-time neural MIDI
performance model with constraints for pitch, time, instrument, and sampling,
and it can be trained from MIDI. It may provide a useful generic playing
vocabulary quickly. nooise still supplies chord constraints, current scale,
and the one-bar-ahead scheduler. Notochord alone does not give nooise's
explicit harmonic state or a durable motif contract, so it is a benchmark and
possible fine-tuning starting point, not the final architecture by default.

### Notochord loopback proof of concept

The first live proof of concept is complete. nooise sent its Lead MIDI to a
virtual MIDI bus, Notochord heard that player stream and generated an answer,
and nooise received the generated MIDI back on a separate channel. The result
was audible in the running app.

This proved the narrow end-to-end seam: nooise can exchange live MIDI with a
real-time improviser without routing its own output back as fresh player
input. It did not prove chord awareness from nooise's progression state,
prompt control, long-range motif development, or the quality of Notochord's
generated playing. Those are the reasons it remains a baseline in this plan,
not the AI Lead architecture.

MIDI-GPT and similar bar-oriented symbolic models are candidates for offline
phrase generation, filling a gap, or proposing several next-bar choices. They
are less naturally suited to an event-by-event player that must react in a
few milliseconds.

The custom path is intentionally narrow:

1. Encode a note event with pitch relative to the current chord and home
   scale, onset delta, duration, velocity, and expressive timing offset.
2. Condition it on the current nooise harmony, transport position,
   arrangement energy, structured brief, recent notes, and planner decision.
3. Train a small causal event model on human takes, with variable-length
   windows that can cover at least a whole phrase. Retain full session
   boundaries and feed motif summaries across windows.
4. Train the planner from phrase labels and derived phrase boundaries, then
   refine it with starred and rejected examples. It may begin as a simpler
   retrieval and ranking system before becoming a learned model.
5. Add preference tuning only after there is enough explicit listening
   feedback. Do not infer “good” from every note that happened to be played.

Fine-tuning a compatible base model can lower the amount of data needed for
note-level fluency. A custom small model keeps the inputs, output constraints,
and personal style legible. The decision is made by blind audition against the
same charts, not by which training curve looks cleaner.

## Runtime contract

Generation stays outside the audio callback. The model receives a snapshot at
least a bar ahead, proposes a phrase or upcoming events, and a scheduler emits
the approved MIDI on the musical clock. If inference is late, the player hears
silence or the already-scheduled material. They never hear an audio glitch or
an unbounded wait.

nooise is the final musical safety layer. Before scheduling, it verifies:

- MIDI source identity prevents generated notes returning as fresh model
  input;
- output is monophonic unless a future mode explicitly changes that contract;
- pitch lies in the selected Lead reach and current permitted scale or chord
  set, with explicitly authored exceptions for approach notes;
- new notes are valid for their scheduled chord, not only the chord that was
  active during inference;
- note-offs, velocity, and silence are scheduled reliably across a transport
  stop, a chord change, a model timeout, and human takeover.

The human owns the Lead immediately. Playing a MIDI note causes the AI to
yield, cancels unsounded material in the player-owned window, and preserves
the motif record as context. A player can also ask for less or more motion,
skip the next phrase, repeat the current idea, or request an answer. These
actions belong in ordinary Lead browsing and must preserve arrow and Tab
navigation; they do not create a separate performance mode.

Generated output is scheduled one bar ahead but should be cancellable until a
small safety horizon before its event. That leaves the player room to take
over without stranded notes while keeping enough lookahead to survive normal
inference variation.

## Evaluation by listening

Loss and note accuracy are development signals. They do not decide whether
the player is musical.

Maintain a fixed blind-listening suite of nooise charts: major, minor, modal,
borrowed-chord, slow, fast, swung, sparse, and dense. For each chart, compare
human, baseline, and candidate takes with the same seed conditions where a
seed applies. Listeners score, in plain language:

- chord relevance and resolution;
- breathing and useful silence;
- a recognizable idea across several phrases;
- appropriate repeat, variation, and answer behavior;
- register, contour, and velocity that fit the arrangement;
- lack of stuck notes, late notes, awkward leaps, or uncontrolled density;
- whether it invites a human response.

Log every audition with the chart, model and checkpoint, seed, runtime
settings, prompt brief, and listener verdict. A score may improve while the
model becomes busier; the scorecard has separate space and continuity fields
so that failure remains visible.

The first playable acceptance pass is simple: start a lead turn over a known
progression, hear a recurring musical idea across at least two phrase
boundaries, change the brief, then take over on MIDI without a late generated
note or transport interruption. A phrase that is harmonically correct but
cannot leave space or answer the player has not met the goal.

## First milestones

1. Build the opt-in Lead Take Recorder and its raw event schema. Verify it
   captures a MIDI performance with exact nooise harmonic and transport
   context without affecting audio.
2. Build the deterministic compiler, take browser, inclusion decisions, and
   dataset manifest. Listen to compiled MIDI against its original take.
3. Collect and review 25 to 50 hours across the matrix above. Establish a
   held-out listening suite before choosing a model.
4. Run the Notochord baseline and a retrieval-based motif player over the
   same charts. Compare them by blind listening.
5. Train the first custom phrase-conditioned event model. Run it one bar ahead
   through the nooise scheduler with human takeover.
6. Continue collection and iteration at 100, 250, and 1,000 hours, changing
   the collection matrix when listening reveals a gap.

## Open decisions

- The exact on-disk data directory and retention controls need a recorder
  proposal before they become user-facing behavior.
- Quantization must preserve expressive timing while giving the model a stable
  representation. The first compiler should retain raw timing and derive,
  rather than replace it with, a grid-relative view.
- Approach notes and notes outside the home scale need a clear performance
  vocabulary before an output constraint permits them.
- Phrase-boundary detection can begin from bars and rests; a learned segmenter
  is not required for the first data set.
- The first learned planner may be replaced by motif retrieval if retrieval
  sounds more intentional. The musical result decides.
